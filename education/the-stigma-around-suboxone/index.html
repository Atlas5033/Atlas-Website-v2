The Stigma Around Suboxone: Are You Still in Recovery if You Take Medication?

Yes. Taking Suboxone does not mean you are not in recovery.

People taking buprenorphine—commonly prescribed as Suboxone—still hear that they are “replacing one drug with another,” that they aren't really sober, or that they aren't “clean” as long as they take medication.

There are real conversations to have about buprenorphine. It causes physical dependence. It has side effects. Stopping it can be difficult, and patients deserve to understand that before they start treatment.

But none of those things means a person taking buprenorphine cannot be in recovery.

The problem starts when we reduce something as complicated as recovery to one question: Are you taking a medication?

Recovery Is Bigger Than a Prescription

Recovery from opioid use disorder is not one thing. It can mean better physical and mental health, repairing relationships, returning to work, becoming more dependable, reducing harm, finding purpose, and moving away from the compulsive substance use that once controlled someone's life.

SAMHSA describes recovery as a process of change through which people improve their health and wellness, live self-directed lives, and work toward their full potential.

That definition leaves room for different paths.

Imagine someone who takes prescribed buprenorphine every morning, has stopped using fentanyl, returned to work, rebuilt relationships, and is actively working on the parts of life that were damaged during active addiction.

Is that person not in recovery because they take a medication?

Medication status alone doesn't tell us very much about recovery.

Dependence and Addiction Are Not the Same Thing

This is probably the most important distinction in the entire conversation.

Buprenorphine can cause physical dependence.

If you take it regularly, your body adapts to it. If you suddenly stop taking it, you can experience withdrawal.

That is physical dependence.

Addiction is different. It involves a broader pattern of impaired control, compulsive use, continued use despite harmful consequences, and disruption in someone's life.

A person taking a stable dose of prescribed buprenorphine may be physically dependent on the medication while experiencing far less compulsive opioid use, less chaos, greater stability, and lower risk associated with illicit opioid use.

Using the word “addiction” for both situations blurs an important clinical distinction.

For a deeper explanation, see “Is Suboxone Replacing One Addiction With Another?” on the Atlas Recovery Education Center.

Opioid Use Disorder Is a Medical Condition

Opioid use disorder is a treatable medical disorder, not simply a failure of willpower or character.

We accept long-term treatment for medical conditions throughout healthcare. Someone taking insulin for diabetes or medication for high blood pressure isn't expected to stop treatment to prove that they have successfully managed their condition.

Opioid use disorder isn't diabetes or hypertension, and the comparison shouldn't be stretched too far. But the underlying point is the same:

Needing treatment does not mean treatment has failed.

There is also no predetermined point at which someone taking buprenorphine is supposed to be “finished.” Some people take it for months. Others take it for years. For some, long-term treatment makes sense. Others eventually decide with their clinician that they want to taper.

How long someone needs medication is a clinical decision, not a measure of how serious they are about recovery.

What Does “Clean” Actually Mean?

The language around recovery deserves some scrutiny too.

Many people use “clean” to describe their own recovery. If that word has meaning for someone, that's their choice.

Using it to judge someone else's recovery is different.

If a person taking prescribed buprenorphine is “not clean,” what does that make them?

The implication isn't particularly subtle.

Someone can stop using fentanyl, reduce their overdose risk, return to their family, rebuild their life, and take buprenorphine every morning. Describing that person as somehow less legitimate because medication remains part of their treatment tells us more about our definition of recovery than it does about theirs.

What About People Who Had a Bad Experience With Suboxone?

This is where the conversation needs more nuance.

Not every criticism of Suboxone is stigma.

Some people have had genuinely difficult experiences stopping buprenorphine. Some experience side effects. Others started treatment without fully understanding that physical dependence would develop or that treatment might last much longer than they expected.

That frustration shouldn't be dismissed.

Common side effects of Suboxone can include headache, nausea, vomiting, constipation, sweating, and insomnia. Buprenorphine products that dissolve in the mouth have also been associated with dental problems.

And stopping buprenorphine abruptly can cause withdrawal. When someone decides to discontinue treatment, tapering is generally done gradually and with clinical monitoring.

Patients should know these things before they start.

Informed consent shouldn't amount to “this will help your opioid use disorder” followed by a prescription. Patients deserve an honest discussion about benefits, risks, side effects, physical dependence, alternatives, expected duration of treatment, and what eventually stopping medication might involve.

If someone didn't receive that conversation, they have a legitimate criticism of the care they received.

But that's different from saying buprenorphine doesn't work or that taking it is simply another form of addiction.

We don't need to minimize the drawbacks of a medication to recognize its benefits.

Medication Creates Space. Recovery Still Takes Work.

Buprenorphine can reduce withdrawal and cravings and decrease illicit opioid use. Research also shows lower mortality during periods when people remain in treatment compared with periods outside treatment.

Those benefits matter enormously.

But medication doesn't repair a relationship. It doesn't find someone a job, treat every psychiatric condition, rebuild trust, create meaning, or decide what kind of life someone wants to live.

What it can do is create space to recover.

For one person, that space might be used for therapy. For another, work, parenting, exercise, peer support, rebuilding relationships, addressing trauma, finding stable housing, or simply learning how to live without spending every day pursuing opioids or trying not to become sick.

Medication can make that work possible.

It doesn't do the work for you.

There Is More Than One Path Through Recovery

None of this is an argument against abstinence-based recovery.

Twelve-step programs, faith communities, peer support, therapy, recovery groups, and other abstinence-oriented approaches have helped many people build meaningful lives. Some people taking buprenorphine eventually decide they want to live without medication, and that can be a reasonable goal too.

The problem isn't choosing abstinence.

The problem is turning one person's choice into the standard by which everyone else's recovery is judged.

There should be room for different paths.

Ask a Better Question

Maybe the question shouldn't be:

“Are you still taking Suboxone?”

Maybe it should be:

“How is your life changing?”

Are you safer?

Are you healthier?

Are you moving away from compulsive opioid use?

Are you rebuilding relationships and responsibilities?

Are you reconnecting with work, family, community, or purpose?

Are you moving toward the life you want?

Those answers tell us much more about recovery than whether someone takes a medication every morning.

Recovery is not defined by the absence of buprenorphine. It is a process of change—and for many people, medication creates the space in which that change can happen.
