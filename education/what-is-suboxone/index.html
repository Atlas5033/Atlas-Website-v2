What Is Suboxone and How Does It Work?

Suboxone is a prescription medication used to treat opioid use disorder. It combines buprenorphine, which reduces opioid withdrawal and cravings, with naloxone, which is included mainly to discourage certain forms of misuse.

Suboxone does not simply “detox” someone from opioids. It can provide ongoing stability by helping the brain and body adjust without the repeated cycles of withdrawal, craving, and opioid use.

For many people, that stability creates space to focus on health, relationships, work, family, and other personal goals.

Key takeaways

  • Suboxone contains two medications: buprenorphine and naloxone.
  • Buprenorphine reduces withdrawal and cravings by partially activating opioid receptors.
  • Buprenorphine attaches strongly to opioid receptors and can weaken the effects of other opioids.
  • Naloxone is included mainly to discourage certain forms of misuse.
  • Suboxone is generally safer than full opioid agonists, but it is not risk-free.
  • Starting Suboxone at the wrong time can cause sudden withdrawal, so initiation should follow an individualized clinical plan.

What Is in Suboxone?

Suboxone is a brand-name medication containing:

  • Buprenorphine, the ingredient that treats opioid withdrawal and cravings
  • Naloxone, an opioid blocker included mainly to discourage injection misuse

People often use the name “Suboxone” to describe buprenorphine-and-naloxone medications more generally, including generic films and tablets.

The medication is usually allowed to dissolve under the tongue or against the inside of the cheek. It should be taken exactly as prescribed.

Other forms of buprenorphine are also available, including buprenorphine-only tablets and extended-release injections. Those medications contain buprenorphine, but they are not Suboxone.

How Does Buprenorphine Work?

Opioids affect receptors in the brain and body. Fentanyl, heroin, oxycodone, and similar opioids are known as full opioid agonists because they activate these receptors fully.

Buprenorphine works differently. It is a partial opioid agonist. It attaches to the same receptors but activates them less fully.

An appropriate dose can:

  • Relieve opioid withdrawal
  • Reduce cravings
  • Help a person feel physically stable
  • Reduce repeated cycles of intoxication and withdrawal
  • Block or weaken the effects of other opioids

Buprenorphine is also long-acting. Instead of wearing off quickly and triggering another cycle of withdrawal and craving, it can provide steadier receptor activity over time.

The goal is not to produce intoxication. The goal is to help the person feel stable enough to function without being repeatedly pulled back into opioid use.

What Does “Partial Opioid Agonist” Mean?

The term sounds technical, but the basic idea is straightforward.

A full opioid agonist can continue producing stronger effects as the dose increases. Those effects include euphoria, sedation, and slowed breathing.

Buprenorphine activates the receptor only partially. Some of its opioid effects reach a plateau as the dose increases. This is often called a ceiling effect.

The ceiling effect gives buprenorphine a different safety profile from fentanyl, heroin, and other full opioid agonists. In particular, breathing suppression tends to level off more than it does with full agonists.

That does not mean overdose is impossible. Buprenorphine can still cause dangerous sedation and breathing problems, especially when combined with alcohol, benzodiazepines, sleep medications, or other opioids.

Does Suboxone Block Other Opioids?

Buprenorphine attaches very strongly to opioid receptors. Once it is attached, it can make it harder for other opioids to reach and activate those receptors.

As a result, Suboxone may weaken or block some of the effects of fentanyl, heroin, oxycodone, and other opioids.

This protection is important, but it is not absolute. A person should never try to “break through” buprenorphine by taking more opioids. Doing so can result in overdose, particularly as the additional opioids accumulate in the body.

People taking buprenorphine should still have naloxone available and should make sure family members or other trusted people know how to use it.

Why Is Naloxone Included?

Naloxone is an opioid antagonist, meaning it blocks opioid receptors.

When Suboxone is allowed to dissolve under the tongue or against the cheek as directed, relatively little naloxone reaches the bloodstream. Buprenorphine provides most of the treatment effect.

Naloxone is included primarily to discourage injection misuse. If the medication is altered and injected, the naloxone may block opioid effects or trigger withdrawal in someone who is physically dependent on opioids.

The naloxone in Suboxone is different from the naloxone nasal spray used to reverse an overdose. Suboxone should never be used in place of emergency naloxone during a suspected overdose.

Does Suboxone Make Someone Feel High?

A person who is not accustomed to opioids may feel significant opioid effects from buprenorphine. That is one reason the medication should never be shared or taken without medical guidance.

For someone with opioid tolerance who is taking an appropriate prescribed dose, the treatment goal is not intoxication or euphoria. Most patients describe feeling more normal or steady, with less withdrawal and fewer cravings.

If Suboxone causes severe sleepiness, confusion, slurred speech, poor coordination, or other signs of impairment, the person should contact the prescribing clinician. Severe sedation or trouble breathing requires emergency help.

Why Can Starting Suboxone Cause Sudden Withdrawal?

Because buprenorphine attaches strongly to opioid receptors, it can displace other opioids that are already present.

If buprenorphine is taken too soon after fentanyl, heroin, oxycodone, or another full opioid agonist, it can cause a sudden drop in opioid activity. This can lead to precipitated withdrawal—an abrupt and sometimes severe onset of withdrawal symptoms.

Traditional Suboxone initiation usually begins after clear withdrawal symptoms have developed. The timing varies according to the opioid used, when it was last used, the person’s physical condition, and other clinical factors.

Fentanyl can make timing less predictable. Some patients may need a different initiation strategy.

Do not use someone else’s instructions or medication to start Suboxone. A clinician should help determine an appropriate plan based on your circumstances.

How Is Suboxone Taken?

Buprenorphine-and-naloxone is commonly available as a film or tablet that dissolves:

  • Under the tongue
  • Against the inside of the cheek, when the specific product is approved for buccal use

The medication should be allowed to dissolve completely. It should not be chewed or swallowed like a regular tablet because the body will not absorb it in the same way.

Follow the instructions for your specific medication. Different products may have different directions and are not always interchangeable at the same dose.

After the medication has fully dissolved, gently rinse your teeth and gums with water and swallow. Wait at least one hour before brushing your teeth. These steps can help reduce the risk of dental problems.

What Are the Common Side Effects?

Possible side effects include:

  • Headache
  • Nausea or vomiting
  • Constipation
  • Increased sweating
  • Difficulty sleeping
  • Dizziness or drowsiness
  • Mouth irritation or numbness
  • Symptoms of opioid withdrawal, particularly if treatment is started too soon

Tell your clinician about side effects, especially if they interfere with daily life or make it difficult to continue treatment. The dose, timing, formulation, or other parts of the treatment plan may need to be adjusted.

Buprenorphine products that dissolve in the mouth have also been associated with cavities, tooth damage, and other dental problems. These problems have occurred even in people without a previous history of dental disease.

Regular dental care and the rinsing instructions described above can help reduce risk. Report tooth pain, sensitivity, gum problems, or other dental changes to a dentist and the prescribing clinician.

How Does Suboxone Compare With Methadone and Naltrexone?

Buprenorphine, methadone, and naltrexone are all FDA-approved medications for opioid use disorder, but they work differently.

Buprenorphine

Buprenorphine is a partial opioid agonist. It reduces withdrawal and cravings and can be prescribed in an outpatient medical practice when clinically appropriate.

Methadone

Methadone is a full opioid agonist. It can be highly effective for opioid use disorder but is generally provided for that purpose through a federally certified opioid treatment program.

Naltrexone

Naltrexone is an opioid antagonist. It blocks opioid receptors rather than activating them. A person usually must complete opioid withdrawal before beginning extended-release naltrexone.

No single medication is best for everyone. The right choice depends on medical history, current opioid use, previous treatment experiences, access, preferences, and individual goals.

Is Suboxone Safe?

Suboxone has an established role in treating opioid use disorder and is generally safer than uncontrolled use of fentanyl, heroin, and other full opioid agonists. However, it remains a potent prescription medication.

Important precautions include:

  • Take it only as prescribed.
  • Do not give it to another person.
  • Store it securely and out of sight and reach of children.
  • Tell your clinician about all medications, supplements, alcohol use, and other substances.
  • Do not suddenly stop buprenorphine or another prescribed medication without medical guidance.
  • Keep naloxone available.

Combining buprenorphine with alcohol, benzodiazepines, sleep medications, or other sedatives can increase the risk of dangerous sedation, overdose, and death.

Taking a benzodiazepine does not automatically mean someone cannot receive buprenorphine. The risks of combining the medications must be managed carefully, while the serious risks of leaving opioid use disorder untreated must also be considered.

When to Seek Emergency Help

Call 911 if someone:

  • Cannot be awakened
  • Is breathing slowly, irregularly, or not at all
  • Has blue, purple, or gray lips or fingertips
  • Makes choking, snoring, or gurgling sounds and cannot be awakened
  • May be experiencing an opioid overdose

Give naloxone if it is available. Stay with the person and follow the emergency dispatcher’s instructions.

Seek urgent medical attention for severe sedation, trouble breathing, facial or throat swelling, or another possible serious medication reaction.

If you are experiencing suicidal thoughts or an emotional crisis, call or text 988. Atlas Recovery is not an emergency or crisis service.

The Bottom Line

Suboxone combines buprenorphine and naloxone to treat opioid use disorder.

Buprenorphine partially activates opioid receptors, helping reduce withdrawal and cravings without producing the same pattern of effects as fentanyl, heroin, or other full opioid agonists. Naloxone is included mainly to discourage certain forms of misuse.

Suboxone can provide meaningful protection and stability, but it is not risk-free. Treatment should be individualized, monitored, and adjusted according to the patient’s response and goals.
