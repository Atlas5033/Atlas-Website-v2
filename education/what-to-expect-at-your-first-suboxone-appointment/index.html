What to Expect at Your First Suboxone Appointment

Your first Suboxone appointment is a chance to talk about what has been happening, ask questions, and make a treatment plan with a clinician.

Suboxone contains buprenorphine and naloxone and is used to treat opioid use disorder.

You can expect questions about opioid use, your health, and previous treatment—but the conversation should also cover your strengths, struggles, and what you want your life to look like. You do not need a perfectly organized history or the “right” answers to begin.

What Should You Have Ready?

A few details can make the visit easier:

  • Your current medications, including supplements
  • Information about previous treatment, if available
  • Your preferred pharmacy and insurance information
  • Questions or concerns you want to discuss

For a telehealth appointment, choose a private place where you can speak openly and check your camera, microphone, and internet connection. Ask the practice about any forms or identification needed beforehand.

You do not need every medical record in hand before reaching out.

What Will the Clinician Ask?

Your clinician will ask about the opioids you use, when you last used them, withdrawal symptoms, and previous treatment. They will also review your medical and mental health history, medications, other substance use, and any previous overdoses. These details help guide safe treatment. SAMHSA’s assessment guidance

But the appointment should go beyond a medical history. Your clinician should take time to understand your strengths, what you are struggling with, what has helped before, and what matters to you.

Practical circumstances—such as work, housing, relationships, and support—also shape the plan. The goal is to decide together what treatment fits your needs and your life.

If you have already been taking buprenorphine, including medication that was not prescribed to you, let your clinician know how much you take, when you last took it, and how it has affected you.

Concerns about medication or a previous treatment experience belong in this conversation, too.

Why Discuss Alcohol and Other Medications?

Alcohol, benzodiazepines such as Xanax or Klonopin, sleep medications, and other sedating substances can affect treatment safety.

Combining these substances with buprenorphine can increase the risk of dangerous sedation and breathing problems. Taking a benzodiazepine does not automatically rule out buprenorphine treatment, but it requires careful assessment and medication management. FDA safety communication on benzodiazepines (2020)

Being open about what you take helps your clinician plan with you. Do not abruptly stop a prescribed medication to prepare for the appointment; ask for guidance.

Do You Need to Be in Withdrawal Before the Appointment?

Do not assume you need to arrive in withdrawal. Ask the practice for instructions before changing your medication or opioid use.

The evaluation and the first dose are separate steps. Your clinician can discuss your history and options before deciding when and how you should begin medication.

When starting buprenorphine after other opioids, timing matters. Taking it too soon can cause precipitated withdrawal, a sudden worsening of withdrawal symptoms. The starting plan should account for what you have been using and your clinical condition. Buprenorphine prescribing information

If you are already taking buprenorphine and continuing treatment with a new provider, explain your current regimen so the clinician can plan accordingly.

You should receive clear instructions rather than be left to figure out the timing yourself.

Will You Need a Drug Test?

Your clinician may request a urine drug screen or other laboratory testing. What is needed depends on the practice and your clinical situation. SAMHSA’s Buprenorphine Quick Start Guide

Testing should help guide care—not serve as a judgment about your character or commitment to recovery. You can ask what is being tested, why it is useful, and how the results will affect treatment.

Will You Get a Prescription That Day?

You may receive a prescription after the evaluation if buprenorphine is appropriate. Whether that happens the same day depends on the assessment and whether additional information or care is needed.

Ask the practice beforehand how it handles initial prescriptions. Pharmacy availability and insurance requirements can also affect when you obtain the medication.

Before the visit ends, you should understand the next step and whom to contact if there is a problem.

What Should Be Explained Before You Start?

You deserve a clear explanation of the medication’s expected benefits, risks, alternatives, and what ongoing treatment may involve.

That discussion should include possible side effects, such as constipation, nausea, and headache; physical dependence and withdrawal if medication is stopped; medication interactions; and safe storage. Your clinician should also discuss overdose prevention, including access to naloxone. SAMHSA’s patient education guidance

Useful questions include:

  • How will we know whether the medication is working?
  • What should I do if I feel worse after starting?
  • Which side effects should prompt a call?
  • How will we make decisions about treatment duration or a future taper?
  • What other treatment options should I consider?

If you previously felt unprepared for side effects, dependence, or withdrawal, say so. That experience should help guide the discussion now.

What Should You Know by the End of the Appointment?

By the end of the appointment, you should understand:

  • The recommended treatment plan and why it fits your situation
  • How and when to take medication, if prescribed
  • When you will follow up
  • Whom to contact with questions, worsening symptoms, or difficulty obtaining medication

The first appointment does not need to resolve every part of recovery. You should have time to ask questions, discuss your concerns, and understand the next step.
