Starting Suboxone After Fentanyl: What You Need to Know

If you've been using fentanyl and are considering Suboxone, you may have heard that starting the medication can suddenly make withdrawal worse. Maybe you've experienced that yourself.

Why is fentanyl different?

Suboxone contains buprenorphine, a medication that attaches strongly to opioid receptors in the brain. It can reduce withdrawal symptoms and cravings, helping people move away from unpredictable opioid use.

Fentanyl is a powerful synthetic opioid. With repeated use, fentanyl is thought to build up in fatty tissue and be released slowly over time. As a result, its effects on starting buprenorphine can be harder to predict than with some other opioids.

If you're unfamiliar with buprenorphine, our existing article about how Suboxone works explains the medication in more detail.

What is precipitated withdrawal?

Precipitated withdrawal occurs when buprenorphine displaces another opioid from receptors. Because buprenorphine only partially activates those receptors, opioid effects can drop quickly and trigger withdrawal.

Instead of relieving withdrawal, the first dose can cause symptoms to become suddenly and significantly worse.

Symptoms may include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, sweating, restlessness, anxiety, and body aches.

Although fentanyl can complicate treatment initiation, many people begin buprenorphine successfully without experiencing precipitated withdrawal. [1]

If your symptoms get much worse after your first dose, do not take fentanyl or other opioids on your own to try to relieve them. Seek medical guidance right away. Follow the instructions in your treatment plan for getting help, including outside office hours. Precipitated withdrawal can be treated, but the next step depends on your situation. If you cannot keep fluids down, seek urgent medical care. For chest pain, confusion, trouble breathing, or other severe symptoms, call 911 or go to an emergency department.

Can you still start Suboxone if you've been using fentanyl?

Yes. For many patients, we begin treatment once sufficient opioid withdrawal has developed, rather than simply waiting a fixed number of hours.

We will ask about your most recent opioid use, current withdrawal symptoms, previous buprenorphine treatment, and whether you've experienced precipitated withdrawal before.

Some patients can begin using a traditional withdrawal-based approach. There are other ways to start, including approaches that use very small doses or take place in a monitored setting. These require an individualized plan, and some are better suited to in-person care. At Atlas, we can discuss whether starting through telehealth is appropriate or help you find a program that can provide the care you need.

The best approach depends on your medical history, current opioid use, and treatment setting. For some people, methadone through an opioid treatment program is a better fit. [2]

What about starting treatment at home?

Some people can begin buprenorphine at home with telehealth guidance and a clear medication plan. [3]

When appropriate, we may also prescribe medication to help manage withdrawal symptoms while you prepare to start buprenorphine.

Before you start, we will explain when to take the first dose, how to recognize withdrawal symptoms, what to do if symptoms worsen, and where to get help during and outside office hours.

Others may need to start in person or in a higher level of care. Atlas can help you find an appropriate program.

Keep naloxone (Narcan) on hand while you are starting treatment.

Do not attempt to start, restart, or change your dose of buprenorphine using general dosing instructions found online. The appropriate timing and dose depend on your individual circumstances.

What if you've experienced precipitated withdrawal before?

If you've experienced precipitated withdrawal, tell us before starting treatment again.

We can discuss what happened and what medications you were taking, then assess whether restarting through telehealth is appropriate or whether a referral would be a better fit.

A previous difficult experience doesn't necessarily mean buprenorphine won't work for you.
