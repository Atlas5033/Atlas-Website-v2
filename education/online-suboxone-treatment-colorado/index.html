How to Get Suboxone Online in Colorado: What to Expect

If you are looking for Suboxone treatment in Colorado, you may be able to start treatment through telehealth without traveling to a clinic.

The process is usually more straightforward than many people expect. You meet with a licensed clinician, complete an evaluation for opioid use disorder, discuss whether buprenorphine is appropriate for you, and—when appropriate—a prescription can be sent electronically to your pharmacy.

You do not need to have everything figured out before making the first appointment.

Step 1: Find a Provider Who Treats Opioid Use Disorder

Suboxone is a brand name for a medication containing buprenorphine and naloxone. Buprenorphine is used to treat opioid use disorder by reducing withdrawal and cravings and helping protect against overdose.

Not every medical or psychiatric provider prescribes buprenorphine, so the first step is finding a clinician who specifically treats opioid use disorder.

For telehealth treatment, the provider must also be able to legally provide care where you are physically located during your appointment.

At Atlas Recovery, treatment is provided by telehealth to patients located throughout Colorado.

Step 2: Complete an Evaluation

Before prescribing buprenorphine, your clinician needs to understand what is actually going on.

An initial evaluation will usually include questions about:

  • The opioids you are currently using and when you last used them
  • Withdrawal symptoms and cravings
  • Previous treatment with Suboxone, methadone, or other medications
  • Alcohol and other substance use
  • Current medications
  • Medical and psychiatric history
  • Previous overdoses
  • Your goals for treatment and recovery

Depending on the practice and your individual situation, the evaluation may also include laboratory testing or a urine drug screen.

Drug testing should not be about passing or failing a test. When it is used, the purpose is to give you and your clinician better information about what is in your system and how to provide treatment safely.

Step 3: Make a Plan for Starting Buprenorphine

If buprenorphine is appropriate, you and your clinician will decide how to start it.

Some people are already taking buprenorphine and simply need to continue treatment with a new provider. Others are starting for the first time or returning to treatment after using fentanyl, heroin, prescription opioids, or another opioid.

Starting buprenorphine requires some planning because taking a standard dose too soon after certain opioids can trigger precipitated withdrawal—a rapid and very uncomfortable onset of withdrawal symptoms.

For many patients, buprenorphine can be started at home with clear instructions and a plan for what to do if problems arise. The right approach depends on what you have been using, how recently you used it, your withdrawal symptoms, and other clinical factors.

You should not have to figure this out on your own.

Step 4: Your Prescription Is Sent to a Pharmacy

When buprenorphine treatment is appropriate, the prescription can generally be sent electronically to a pharmacy in Colorado.

Sometimes the pharmacy becomes an unexpected part of the process. A pharmacy may not have the medication in stock, an insurance plan may require additional information, or the pharmacist may need to contact the prescriber before filling the prescription.

If that happens, contact your treatment provider rather than assuming treatment cannot move forward. The next step may be resolving an insurance issue, answering a pharmacy question, or finding a pharmacy that can fill the prescription.

Step 5: Follow Up and Adjust the Treatment

Getting the prescription is the beginning of treatment, not the end of it.

Early follow-up helps determine whether the medication is adequately controlling withdrawal and cravings and whether you are experiencing side effects or other problems.

Over time, treatment may also include medication adjustments, drug testing, overdose-prevention planning, mental health treatment when appropriate, and coordination with therapists, primary care providers, or others involved in your care.

There is no single treatment plan that fits everyone.

Do You Have to Go to a Clinic in Person?

Current as of September 2026: Federal telemedicine flexibilities currently allow buprenorphine for opioid use disorder to be prescribed through telehealth without first completing an in-person medical evaluation when federal prescribing requirements are met.

These federal flexibilities are scheduled to remain in effect through December 31, 2026. Telemedicine regulations have changed several times in recent years and may change again.

Individual providers may also require in-person medical care, laboratory testing, or other evaluations when clinically appropriate.

If you are reading this article after 2026, check the current requirements rather than assuming the rules described here are still in effect.

Can You Use Insurance for Online Suboxone Treatment?

Often, yes. Coverage depends on the provider, your insurance plan, and your individual benefits.

Atlas Recovery accepts several commercial insurance plans and Colorado Medicaid coverage, and self-pay treatment is also available. Coverage, deductibles, copayments, coinsurance, and prior-authorization requirements vary by plan.

It is reasonable to ask about expected costs before beginning treatment.

Is Online Treatment Right for Everyone?

No.

Telehealth can remove major barriers to treatment, particularly for people who live far from a provider or have difficulty leaving work, arranging childcare, or traveling regularly for appointments.

But convenience is not the same thing as clinical appropriateness.

Some situations require a higher level of care, an in-person medical evaluation, emergency treatment, or services that cannot be safely provided through telehealth. A responsible telehealth practice should be willing to tell you when online treatment is not enough.

The First Step Can Be Simple

You do not need to decide what the rest of recovery will look like before asking for help.

The first step may simply be talking with a clinician, explaining what has been happening, and finding out what your options are.
