Can You Restart Suboxone After a Return to Opioid Use?

Maybe you took Suboxone in the past and stopped. Maybe treatment was going well, but life became complicated. Or perhaps you've returned to opioid use and aren't sure what to do next.

For many people, restarting buprenorphine is an option.

Can you take Suboxone again?

In many cases, yes. Buprenorphine can be effective for people who have previously taken it, including those who have returned to opioid use after stopping treatment.

If you've stopped Suboxone, especially after a break or a period of reduced opioid use, your tolerance may be lower than you think. The drug supply is also unpredictable. Keep naloxone (Narcan) with you, avoid using alone, and make sure someone nearby knows how to use it. If someone may be overdosing, give naloxone and call 911. [1]

Restarting medication requires an updated assessment. We will ask about recent opioid use, current tolerance, other medications, medical history, and previous experiences with buprenorphine.

If you've recently used fentanyl or other opioids, restarting Suboxone too soon can cause precipitated withdrawal.

If you have leftover Suboxone from a previous prescription, check with a clinician before taking it. The timing and dose from your old prescription may not fit your current circumstances. Our companion article, Starting Suboxone After Fentanyl: What You Need to Know, explains precipitated withdrawal in more detail.

What if you're still taking Suboxone but have returned to opioid use?

Returning to opioid use while taking prescribed buprenorphine is different from restarting medication after stopping it.

If you're still taking Suboxone, we can reassess your cravings, medication dose, whether you've been able to take it as prescribed, stressors, and whether your current level of care is meeting your needs.

A return to opioid use does not automatically mean buprenorphine treatment should be stopped. [2]

Sometimes adjusting the treatment plan is more helpful than stopping medication. Depending on our assessment, that may include a higher dose to better control cravings and withdrawal. [3]

If Suboxone did not work well last time

It's worth talking about what happened.

Maybe the medication helped with cravings but caused uncomfortable side effects. Perhaps appointments were difficult to attend, the dose wasn't adequately controlling symptoms, or treatment didn't fit your circumstances.

Some people stop because they're feeling better and think they no longer need medication. Others encounter insurance, pharmacy, financial, or scheduling barriers.

Understanding what worked and what didn't can help us decide what to do differently.

In some circumstances, another medication or a more intensive treatment setting may be more appropriate.

Do you have to start over?

We will need to understand your current circumstances, and some parts of the evaluation or medication initiation process may need to be repeated for safety.

Your previous treatment experience can help guide decisions about medication, follow-up, and additional support.

What if you're embarrassed about returning to treatment?

It can be difficult to reach out after returning to opioid use, especially if you're worried about being judged or disappointing someone.

At Atlas Recovery, we want to understand what's happening now and help you decide what to do next.

You can talk openly about what happened, what was difficult, and what you'd like to change.
