How Long Should You Stay on Suboxone?

“How long are you going to stay on Suboxone?”

I hear versions of this question at least as often from people around someone in recovery as I do from patients themselves.

There can be an assumption that Suboxone is supposed to be temporary—that eventually someone should be “ready” to stop taking it. In practice, my patients are more likely to come to me when they have already started thinking about stopping and want to talk through whether the time is right.

There is no standard expiration date for buprenorphine treatment.

Some people eventually decide to taper. Others remain on medication for years or longer. The right duration depends on the person, how treatment is going, and whether the medication continues to support the life and recovery they are trying to build.

Is Suboxone Supposed to Be Short-Term?

Not necessarily.

There is no point in recovery when someone automatically needs to stop buprenorphine. If the medication is helping someone stay stable and supporting their recovery, there may be good reasons to continue it.

I think a better goal than simply “getting off Suboxone” is recovery itself: better health, better quality of life, meaningful relationships, stability, and the ability to do the things that matter to you.

Medication should support those goals.

If it is doing that, there may be good reasons to continue it. If someone is experiencing side effects, feels that the medication is creating problems of its own, or simply wants to explore life without it, considering a taper is reasonable too.

The question is not whether staying on medication or getting off medication is the “right” choice. It is whether the medication continues to do more good than harm for that particular person.

Why Not Just Stop Once You Feel Better?

Because sometimes the treatment helping you feel better is part of what is keeping things stable.

Stopping buprenorphine can increase the risk of returning to opioid use, particularly if it is stopped too quickly or before someone is ready. A return to opioid use after a period of reduced use can also be dangerous because opioid tolerance may have decreased.

That does not mean someone can never stop Suboxone. It means feeling better is not, by itself, a reason that you need to stop.

How Do You Know When It Might Be Time to Taper?

There is no checklist that can tell someone exactly when the time is right.

I would want to look at the bigger picture.

How stable has your recovery been? What is happening with cravings? How is your mental health? What does your support system look like? Are housing, work, and relationships reasonably stable? Are you having side effects or other problems related to the medication? Why do you want to stop? And what would we do if cravings or opioid use returned?

Those questions tell us much more than the number of months or years someone has been taking Suboxone.

If you decide to taper, it should generally be planned rather than rushed. There is no reason to turn tapering into another test you have to pass. The pace can be adjusted based on how you are doing along the way.

What If You Are Not Ready to Stop?

Then there may be no reason to.

Remaining on buprenorphine does not mean someone has failed at recovery, and taking medication does not make recovery less legitimate.

We have gone into those questions more deeply in The Stigma Around Suboxone: Are You Still in Recovery if You Take Medication? and Is Suboxone Replacing One Addiction With Another? Those articles discuss addiction, physical dependence, and some of the stigma that still surrounds medication treatment.

So, How Long Should You Stay on Suboxone?

There is no predetermined length of time.

Continue to ask whether the medication is helping you stay healthy, avoid returning to opioid use, and build the life you want. Weigh those benefits against side effects, other consequences of treatment, and your own goals with your clinician.

For some people, treatment may eventually include a gradual taper. For others, continuing buprenorphine for years or longer may make sense.

Neither decision is a measure of how serious you are about recovery.

The goal is not to stay on Suboxone or to get off Suboxone. The goal is recovery, health, and quality of life. Medication is there to support those things.
