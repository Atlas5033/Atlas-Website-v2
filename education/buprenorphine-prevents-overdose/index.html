How Buprenorphine Helps Prevent Overdose and Save Lives

Buprenorphine lowers the risk of opioid overdose and death by reducing withdrawal and cravings, decreasing uncontrolled opioid use, and providing more stable opioid-receptor activity.

Buprenorphine lowers the risk of opioid overdose and death by reducing withdrawal and cravings, decreasing uncontrolled opioid use, and providing more stable opioid-receptor activity.

The protection is meaningful. Major studies have found substantially lower rates of fatal overdose and death among people receiving buprenorphine treatment.

However, buprenorphine does not make overdose impossible. Naloxone, safer-use planning, and continued access to treatment remain important.

Key takeaways

  • Buprenorphine treatment is associated with lower rates of opioid overdose and death.
  • A large study found a 62% lower risk of opioid-involved overdose death among Medicare beneficiaries who received buprenorphine after surviving a nonfatal overdose.
  • Buprenorphine reduces withdrawal and cravings, which can reduce exposure to fentanyl and other unpredictable opioids.
  • Its strong attachment to opioid receptors may weaken the effects of other opioids, but this protection is not complete.
  • Overdose risk can increase when treatment is interrupted or stopped.
  • People taking buprenorphine should still keep naloxone available.

What Do the Numbers Show?

Researchers have studied buprenorphine in different populations and treatment settings. Although the exact numbers vary, the overall finding is consistent: people are less likely to die while receiving effective medication treatment for opioid use disorder.

After a Nonfatal Overdose

A 2023 study followed more than 81,000 Medicare beneficiaries who had survived a nonfatal opioid overdose.

Receiving buprenorphine afterward was associated with a 62% lower risk of opioid-involved overdose death during the following year.

This result applies to the population studied and does not predict an individual patient’s outcome. Still, it demonstrates a substantial association between receiving buprenorphine after an overdose and surviving the following year.

During and Outside Treatment

A major systematic review published in The BMJ compared death rates during and outside methadone or buprenorphine treatment.

In the buprenorphine cohort included in the review:

  • The fatal-overdose rate was 1.4 deaths per 1,000 person-years during treatment
  • The rate was 4.6 deaths per 1,000 person-years outside treatment

The observed fatal-overdose rate during treatment was about 70% lower than the rate outside treatment.

The same cohort had:

  • 4.3 deaths from all causes per 1,000 person-years during treatment
  • 9.5 deaths per 1,000 person-years outside treatment

That represents an observed all-cause death rate approximately 55% lower during treatment.

A “person-year” is a research measure representing one person followed for one year. Researchers use it to compare rates across large groups of people followed for different lengths of time.

Compared With Other Treatment Pathways

A study of more than 40,000 adults with opioid use disorder compared six treatment pathways.

Treatment with either buprenorphine or methadone was associated with:

  • A 76% lower risk of overdose at three months
  • A 59% lower risk of overdose at 12 months

Buprenorphine and methadone were the only pathways in that study associated with significantly lower overdose risk at both time points.

These studies were observational. They show strong associations but cannot prove that medication alone caused every difference. However, the consistency of findings across different studies and populations supports buprenorphine as a lifesaving treatment.

How Does Buprenorphine Lower Overdose Risk?

Buprenorphine provides several layers of protection.

It Reduces Withdrawal and Cravings

Opioid withdrawal can cause pain, nausea, diarrhea, sweating, anxiety, restlessness, insomnia, and a powerful urge to use opioids for relief.

Buprenorphine helps stabilize opioid receptors, relieve withdrawal, and reduce cravings. When someone is no longer trying to escape withdrawal every few hours, the pressure to return to fentanyl, heroin, or another opioid may decrease.

This also reduces exposure to an unpredictable drug supply in which fentanyl and other potent synthetic opioids may be present in unknown amounts.

It Provides More Stable Opioid-Receptor Activity

Fentanyl, heroin, and many prescription opioids are full opioid agonists. They can strongly activate opioid receptors and suppress the brain’s drive to breathe.

Buprenorphine is a partial opioid agonist. It activates the same receptors less fully and has a ceiling effect for some opioid effects, including respiratory suppression.

This gives buprenorphine a wider margin of safety than full opioid agonists, although overdose remains possible.

It Can Weaken the Effects of Other Opioids

Buprenorphine attaches strongly to opioid receptors. Once attached, it can make it harder for fentanyl, heroin, oxycodone, and other opioids to activate those receptors.

This may weaken some of their effects and provide a degree of protection if another opioid is used.

That protection is not complete. A person should never try to overcome buprenorphine’s blocking effect by taking larger amounts of another opioid. Larger amounts can overcome some of the protective effect and cause a fatal overdose.

Why Staying in Treatment Matters

Buprenorphine’s benefits are strongest while a person remains engaged in treatment.

Treatment interruptions can lead to:

  • Return of withdrawal and cravings
  • Renewed exposure to fentanyl or other opioids
  • Reduced opioid tolerance
  • Increased overdose risk
  • Difficulty reconnecting with care

Risk may be especially high after medication is stopped and opioid tolerance has decreased. An amount of fentanyl, heroin, or another opioid that a person previously used may become fatal after time away from it.

Stopping buprenorphine is not automatically the wrong decision. Some people eventually choose to taper after careful discussion with their clinician.

The important point is that stopping should be planned rather than driven by stigma, outside pressure, medication-access problems, or an arbitrary deadline.

Does Buprenorphine Make Overdose Impossible?

No.

Buprenorphine lowers risk, but no medication eliminates it completely. Overdose can still occur when:

  • Buprenorphine is combined with fentanyl, heroin, or other opioids
  • Someone tries to overcome its blocking effects with larger opioid amounts
  • It is combined with alcohol, benzodiazepines, sleep medications, or other sedatives
  • It is taken by someone without opioid tolerance
  • The medication is used differently from how it was prescribed
  • Treatment is interrupted and the person returns to opioid use after losing tolerance

Treatment should include practical information about medication interactions, naloxone, overdose recognition, secure storage, and what to do if opioid use occurs.

Why Is Naloxone Still Important?

Naloxone is a medication that can rapidly reverse an opioid overdose.

People taking buprenorphine should still have naloxone because:

  • Buprenorphine does not completely block other opioids
  • Fentanyl may be present unexpectedly
  • Sedative combinations can increase overdose risk
  • Treatment interruptions can occur
  • A family member, friend, or another person nearby may need it

Naloxone generally will not harm someone if opioids are not the cause of the emergency. If an overdose is suspected, give naloxone and call 911.

More than one dose may be needed. Stay with the person until emergency help arrives.

What If Someone Uses Opioids During Treatment?

Using an opioid during treatment should prompt a safety conversation—not automatic shame, punishment, or discharge.

A recurrence of opioid use may mean that:

  • The buprenorphine dose needs to be reassessed
  • Withdrawal or cravings are not fully controlled
  • The person has missed doses or had trouble obtaining medication
  • A mental health concern or stressful situation needs attention
  • The treatment plan does not fit the person’s current needs
  • Additional support would be helpful

Ending treatment in response to opioid use can remove an important layer of overdose protection at the time it may be needed most.

The safer response is to understand what happened, address immediate risks, and adjust the plan collaboratively whenever outpatient treatment remains appropriate.

Common Questions

“If Buprenorphine Blocks Opioids, Why Do I Still Need Naloxone?”

Because the blocking effect is not complete. Potent opioids, large opioid amounts, and combinations with sedatives can still cause an overdose.

Naloxone adds another layer of protection.

“Can I Still Overdose on Buprenorphine?”

Yes, although buprenorphine generally has a safer respiratory profile than full opioid agonists.

The risk increases when it is combined with alcohol, benzodiazepines, sleep medications, other sedatives, or additional opioids. It can also be dangerous for children and people without opioid tolerance.

Store the medication securely and never share it.

When to Seek Emergency Help

Call 911 immediately if someone:

  • Cannot be awakened
  • Is breathing slowly, irregularly, or not at all
  • Has blue, purple, or gray lips or fingertips
  • Makes choking, snoring, or gurgling sounds and cannot be awakened
  • Has severe sleepiness with pinpoint pupils
  • May have taken an opioid and appears to be overdosing

Give naloxone if it is available. If the person does not respond, give another dose according to the product instructions. Stay with the person and follow the emergency dispatcher’s directions.

If you are experiencing suicidal thoughts or an emotional crisis, call or text 988. Atlas Recovery is not an emergency or crisis service.

The Bottom Line

Buprenorphine is one of the most effective tools available for preventing opioid overdose and death.

Research has associated buprenorphine treatment with:

  • A 62% lower risk of opioid-involved overdose death among Medicare beneficiaries receiving buprenorphine after a nonfatal overdose
  • An approximately 70% lower observed fatal-overdose rate during treatment in the buprenorphine cohort included in one major review
  • Substantially lower overdose risk when buprenorphine or methadone treatment is compared with treatment pathways that do not include these medications

These numbers do not promise that an individual person will never experience an overdose. They show that staying connected to effective medication treatment can meaningfully improve the odds of survival.

Buprenorphine works by reducing withdrawal and cravings, providing more stable opioid-receptor activity, and weakening some effects of other opioids. Naloxone and emergency care remain essential because no medication provides complete protection.
