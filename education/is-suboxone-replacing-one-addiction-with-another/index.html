Is Suboxone Replacing One Addiction With Another?

The short answer is no—not when Suboxone is taken as prescribed to treat opioid use disorder.

Aaron Jones, MSN, PMHNP-BCWritten and clinically reviewed
9 min readPublished and clinically reviewed: September 2026

On this page

Suboxone contains buprenorphine, a medication that reduces opioid withdrawal and cravings and helps protect against overdose. Although the body can become physically dependent on buprenorphine, physical dependence is not the same as addiction.

That distinction matters. Addiction involves more than needing a medication or experiencing withdrawal when it is stopped. It involves impaired control, compulsive use, and continued use despite harmful consequences.

A person taking a stable dose of buprenorphine may be physically dependent on the medication while also becoming healthier, safer, and more in control of their life. That is treatment—not simply replacing one addiction with another.

Key takeaways

  • Suboxone is an evidence-based medication used to treat opioid use disorder.
  • Buprenorphine reduces opioid withdrawal and cravings and can weaken the effects of other opioids.
  • Physical dependence can occur during treatment, but dependence is not the same as addiction.
  • Buprenorphine can be misused and is not risk-free, but its effects and safety profile differ from those of fentanyl, heroin, and other full opioid agonists.
  • Medication changes should be planned collaboratively with a qualified clinician.

Why Do People Ask This Question?

Many people have been taught that recovery requires complete abstinence from every opioid medication. Families may worry that taking buprenorphine means a person is still “using.” Patients may also wonder whether needing medication every day means they have traded one problem for another.

These concerns are understandable. Buprenorphine is an opioid medication, and the body can become physically dependent on it.

But the fact that two substances affect the same receptors does not mean they produce the same effects, risks, or patterns of behavior.

The more useful questions are:

  • How is the medication affecting the person?
  • Is it being taken as prescribed?
  • Is it reducing harm or causing harm?
  • Is the person becoming safer and more stable?
  • Is treatment helping the person regain control of their life?

When buprenorphine is working as intended, it interrupts the repeated cycle of withdrawal, craving, opioid use, and temporary relief. That stability can create room for health, safety, and recovery.

Addiction and Physical Dependence Are Not the Same

The terms addiction, physical dependence, and withdrawal are often used as though they mean the same thing. They do not.

What Is Physical Dependence?

Physical dependence means the body has adapted to the presence of a medication or substance. If it is stopped suddenly, withdrawal symptoms may develop.

Dependence can occur with many medications, including some antidepressants, blood-pressure medications, corticosteroids, and medications used for pain or anxiety. Experiencing withdrawal after stopping a medication does not automatically mean someone is addicted to it.

A person who takes buprenorphine regularly will often develop physical dependence. Stopping it abruptly can cause opioid withdrawal. That is one reason medication changes should be planned with a clinician.

What Is Addiction—and How Is It Related to Opioid Use Disorder?

Addiction is a commonly used term for a disease process in which substance use becomes difficult to control and continues despite harmful consequences.

Opioid use disorder is the clinical diagnosis used when this pattern involves opioids and meets established diagnostic criteria. Clinicians assess symptoms such as:

  • Difficulty controlling how much or how often opioids are used
  • Strong urges or cravings
  • Continued use despite health, work, legal, or relationship problems
  • Spending significant time obtaining, using, or recovering from opioids
  • Giving up important activities because of opioid use
  • Using opioids in dangerous situations

Opioid use disorder may be diagnosed as mild, moderate, or severe, depending on the number of symptoms present.

Physical dependence is different. It can occur as part of opioid use disorder, but it can also develop while someone is taking a medication appropriately.

A person may still have a diagnosis of opioid use disorder while stable or in remission during buprenorphine treatment. However, physical dependence on prescribed buprenorphine does not, by itself, mean the person has developed a new addiction to the medication.

How Is Buprenorphine Different From Fentanyl, Heroin, or Prescription Opioids?

Buprenorphine is an opioid, but it does not act exactly like fentanyl, heroin, oxycodone, or other full opioid agonists.

Buprenorphine is a partial opioid agonist. This means it attaches strongly to opioid receptors but activates them less fully than full opioid agonists.

At an appropriate dose, buprenorphine can:

  • Relieve opioid withdrawal
  • Reduce cravings
  • Help a person feel physically stable
  • Block or weaken the effects of other opioids
  • Reduce repeated cycles of intoxication and withdrawal

Buprenorphine also has a ceiling effect for some opioid effects. As the dose increases, breathing suppression tends to level off more than it does with full opioid agonists. This gives buprenorphine a different safety profile, although it does not make the medication risk-free.

The treatment goal is not intoxication or euphoria. It is to help the person feel steady enough to function without being repeatedly pulled back into withdrawal and craving.

Can Suboxone Be Misused?

Yes. Buprenorphine can be misused, shared, or taken in ways other than prescribed. It is a controlled medication and should be stored securely, kept away from children, and never given to another person.

That risk should be taken seriously. However, the possibility that a medication can be misused does not make appropriate medical treatment the same as addiction.

Safe treatment may include regular follow-up, assessment of withdrawal and cravings, review of other medications and substances, appropriate drug testing, naloxone education, and adjustments based on the patient’s response and goals.

Monitoring should help make treatment safer—not shame or punish the person receiving care.

Common Questions and Misconceptions

“Isn’t Buprenorphine Still an Opioid?”

Yes. Buprenorphine is an opioid medication. However, its effects, dosing, safety profile, and medical purpose differ from uncontrolled use of fentanyl, heroin, or prescription opioids.

A medication’s drug class alone does not determine whether its use represents effective treatment or active addiction.

“If I Experience Withdrawal Without It, Doesn’t That Mean I Am Addicted?”

Not necessarily.

Withdrawal indicates physical dependence. Addiction involves a broader pattern that includes impaired control, compulsive use, and continued use despite harm.

Physical dependence can occur even when a medication is being taken appropriately and providing clear benefits.

“Is This Just Substituting One Drug for Another?”

Suboxone does replace the opioid activity that the body has become accustomed to, but it does so in a medically controlled and more stable way. That is not the same as replacing one addiction with another.

The purpose of treatment is to reduce withdrawal, cravings, uncontrolled opioid use, overdose risk, and disruption to daily life. The person is not expected to remain trapped in the same harmful cycle under a different name.

“Does Needing Medication Mean Treatment Has Failed?”

No. Continuing a medication because it is helping does not mean a person has failed.

The important question is whether treatment is improving safety, health, stability, and control over one’s life.

Important safety information

Buprenorphine has a safer respiratory profile than full opioid agonists, but overdose and serious breathing problems can still occur.

The risk increases when it is combined with alcohol, benzodiazepines, sleep medications, other sedatives, fentanyl, heroin, or other opioids.

Tell your clinician about all prescribed medications, nonprescribed substances, supplements, and alcohol use. Honest information helps your clinician create a safer plan.

Taking a benzodiazepine or another sedating medication does not automatically exclude someone from buprenorphine treatment. However, these combinations require careful management. Do not suddenly stop buprenorphine, a benzodiazepine, or another prescribed medication without medical guidance.

Call 911 if someone cannot be awakened, is breathing slowly or not at all, or may be experiencing an overdose. Give naloxone if it is available and stay with the person until emergency help arrives.

If you are experiencing suicidal thoughts or an emotional crisis, call or text 988. Atlas Recovery is not an emergency or crisis service.

The Bottom Line

Suboxone does not simply replace one addiction with another when it is used appropriately to treat opioid use disorder.

Buprenorphine can cause physical dependence and must be prescribed and monitored responsibly. But dependence alone is not addiction.

For many people, buprenorphine reduces withdrawal and cravings, lowers the risk of overdose, and provides the stability needed to move forward.

Treatment should not be measured only by whether someone takes medication. It should be measured by whether the person is safer, healthier, more stable, and better able to build the life they want.
